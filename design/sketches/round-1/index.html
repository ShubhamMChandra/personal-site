Painted words, moods and a woven colophon (stills)

Each painting now has moods by area, the way a painter works some places hard and leaves others alone. Some areas are left bare, with the grey showing. Some are worked over with a second layer in a neighbouring colour. Some are loose, with longer, freer strokes. Each painting also breaks a rule two or three times, on purpose: a small bundle laid across the current, a patch repainted late in another colour, a few strokes in the complementary colour, or one oversized sweep. Colophon is woven now: teal and sage weft, mustard warp going over and under, and the odd rust thread. Each section is painted three times to check it never repeats.

Painted words, a brush that runs out (stills)

Each current is now one drag of a loaded brush. It starts full: wide, saturated and thick. As it travels it runs dry, so the strokes get thinner and break up, and the grey shows through in skips. Long currents get reloaded partway, picking up a little colour from the paint they came out of. The hand also changes speed in patches. Fast passages are long, thin and straight, slow ones short, thick and rich. Colophon’s weave is coming in the next update.

Painted words, strokes that flow (stills)

New strokes, after the painters. Each one bends with its current and carries two or three colours streaked along it. Thick paint catches the light along one edge and casts a thin shadow on the other. Each stroke lands over the tail of the one before, like scales, and its tail goes dry so the grey shows through. Neighbouring strokes share a length and a band of colour, so they move together as one sweep, but their joints are staggered. Darker currents go down first and lighter ones over them. The chosen word now keeps a deep ink with a sliver of ground round its letters, so it reads at a glance while the burst still comes out of it.

How the painters lay a stroke

Close-ups from public-domain scans, from Turner to Sargent, to study texture and flow. Van Gogh’s strokes each carry two or three colours, streaked along their length by the bristles. Their thick paint catches the light on one ridge, and they bend and pack into bundles that move together. Monet’s late water lilies and Sorolla drag dry paint over darker paint, so the colour breaks up and the tails go dry. Cézanne, Signac and Cross lay flat patches and dots: lovely, but closer to what our strokes do now, which is why they don’t flow.

Painted words, from the letters (stills)

Stills for now, so revisions go faster. Each row is one click, at three moments: just after, about a third of the way in, and settled. The paint now starts inside the word. Some of the strokes that make up the letters run out of them as currents, flung outward and then caught by the drift. Every later current is laid beside one already there, so the whole left side grows out of the word. About the first third comes straight from the letters. The word keeps a deeper shade of its colour so it still reads through its own burst. The strokes along a current no longer sit end to end: some overlap, some leave a gap, each sits a little off the line, and most are pressed and lifted rather than round at both ends.

Each word has its own colours. The fill is slower, about six seconds, and is rebuilt from how Van Gogh and generative painters work: strokes laid end to end in rows along currents, many swirls of very different sizes, some closed rings and some spirals, a seam where the flow breaks, a patch or two left unpainted, sometimes one oversized run, and a new sky every click. The colour now flows over the grey across a wide, ragged edge.

Painter’s fill, the version before: the spiral’s way of taking over the whole left side, but varied like Starry Night, with swirls of different sizes and turns, lighter hearts, a wandering current between them, and colour in bands with grey worked through. It fills the way a painter works: a few long gestures first, then areas blocked in here and there, then the light and the bright notes last. On the quieter round 1 ground.

Two quieter takes on how the colour arrives.
