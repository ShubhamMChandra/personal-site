# Coming together: brief

Written 14 October 2026, from his words after the recap:

> lets really push on what else could fit that idea of 'theres some beautiful things but i dont get
> the full picture. i move through the site and it assembles'. for EXAMPLE, theres like that canvas
> brushstroke thing and as you move your cursor over it, it assembles into a lovely design even
> though it felt cluttered before, something more lovely than when it started. and also it should
> have texture, whatrever we choose

## Every sketch in this round
- Starts as beautiful pieces that don't yet make a picture: cluttered, never noise.
- Moving through assembles it. Scrolling does it on a phone; a finger or a cursor does it faster
  where it passes; a tap assembles a wide patch at once. What you've passed stays assembled.
- Stillness also assembles it, slowly, over about a minute.
- The end state is a composition someone would make on purpose, lovelier than the start: the shapes
  and the colour both improve.
- Texture is the material itself, not an overlay.
- His words read completely at rest. Nothing is gated behind the motion.
- No caption names the gesture.

## The sketches
1. Paint (his example). Impasto strokes in his dye colours, turned every which way and a little
   muddied. As you move through, they turn into one flowing painting: warm light around his words,
   cool swirling paint between them.
2. Stills first, at his word ("if it makes revisions faster you can show me static mockups
   first"): before-and-after phone screens for three more materials, each assembling a different way.
   - Thread: warp threads dyed in pieces (ikat, in indigo, madder and turmeric) hang out of register,
     then slide into it, and the pattern appears with ikat's feathered edges.
   - Sand: pale sand on a dark bronze plate; the plate hums and the sand gathers on its still lines
     into one figure (a ring round a four-petalled flower).
   - Leaves: fallen leaves on wet dark stone, laid by hand into a ring graded yellow at the rim to
     deep red at the lip of a hole (after Andy Goldsworthy's rowan leaves, 1987).
   Whichever he likes gets built to move.
